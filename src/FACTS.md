## 1. Education and Licensure

- Admission to a CCE-accredited DC program requires **either a baccalaureate degree, or a minimum of 90 semester credit hours** of prior coursework from a regionally/nationally accredited institution. Where the 90-hour route is used without a bachelor's degree, CCE standards specify a minimum cumulative GPA of 3.0 on a 4.0 scale, or a GPA of 2.75–2.99 combined with at least 24 semester credits in life and physical science courses. A bachelor's degree is therefore **not strictly required** by the accreditor, though most matriculants hold one. [CCE Accreditation Standards PDF, https://www.cce-usa.org/uploads/1/0/6/5/106500339/2025-01_cce_accreditation_standards__current_.pdf, checked 2026-09-03]

- Required prerequisite science coursework typically includes biology, general and organic chemistry, physics, and coursework such as anatomy, physiology, and related life sciences; specific course lists vary somewhat by program. [Wikipedia, Chiropractic education, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiropractic_education, checked 2026-09-03; U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Outlook Handbook: Chiropractors, https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/chiropractors.htm, checked 2026-09-03]

- BLS states admission requires "at least 3 years of undergraduate education," consistent with the 90-semester-hour CCE minimum (90 semester hours is roughly 3 years of full-time study), though most applicants nationally hold a full bachelor's degree already. [BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Program length and hours:** The DC degree is a four-year (full-time equivalent) program. CCE accreditation standards require a minimum of **4,200 instructional hours** (classroom, laboratory, and clinical combined), of which at least **1,000 hours must be in a supervised patient-care (clinical) setting**. [CCE Accreditation Standards PDF, checked 2026-09-03; corroborated by ACA, "Key Facts and Figures About the Chiropractic Profession," https://www.acatoday.org/news-publications/newsroom/key-facts/, checked 2026-09-03]

- The ACA states this classroom-hour total is comparable to MD and DO medical school curricula in raw hours, though the content mix (basic science, diagnosis, manipulation-specific technique, clinical internship) differs substantially from an MD/DO curriculum and is not equivalent training. [ACA Key Facts, checked 2026-09-03] — the comparability claim is an advocacy-organization framing; treat the underlying hour figures (4,200 / 1,000) as the verified fact, and the "comparable to medical school" framing as **CONTESTED** rhetoric rather than an independently adjudicated equivalence.

- **NBCE exam structure** (National Board of Chiropractic Examiners; nearly all US states require passage of NBCE Parts for licensure):
  - **Part I** — Basic sciences, computer-based, 255 questions across two sessions: General Anatomy/Spinal Anatomy/Physiology, and Chemistry/Pathology/Microbiology. Students become eligible to sit Part I in their **second year** of the DC program (registrar approval required). [NBCE, Part I Exam, https://www.mynbce.org/part-i/, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **Part II** — Clinical sciences, computer-based. Six domains: General Diagnosis (19%), Neuromusculoskeletal Diagnosis (20%), Diagnostic Imaging (17%), Principles of Chiropractic (14%), Chiropractic Practice (17%), Associated Clinical Sciences (13%). Taken later in the program, generally as students enter clinical training; exact timing not specified by NBCE beyond eligibility rules. [NBCE, Part II Test Plan, https://www.mynbce.org/part-ii-test-plan/, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **Part III** — Case management/clinical competency, computer-based, 130 questions (80 multiple choice, 20 extended multiple-choice case presentations requiring 2 responses each, 30 diagnostic imaging interpretation items). Nine domains including case history, physical exam, neuromusculoskeletal exam, diagnostic imaging, chiropractic techniques, and case management. Candidates must be **within nine months of graduation** and must have already passed Part I. Passing score is 375 (scaled). [NBCE, Part III Exam, https://www.mynbce.org/pt-iii/, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **Part IV** — Practical (hands-on) skills exam, taken **near the end of the program**, eligibility opening six months before graduation. Consists of seven Case Management stations (2-minute chart review, 14-minute patient encounter, 6-minute SOAP note) and one Technique station (2-minute chart review, 14-minute encounter performing 10 adjustment setups). All encounters are video recorded and scored on safety, positioning, contact points, stabilization, and verbalized intent. [NBCE, Part IV Exam, https://www.mynbce.org/part-iv-exam/, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **Physiotherapy (PHT)** — A separate, optional exam (required by some but not all states for a physiotherapy/modalities scope). 90 multiple-choice questions, 75 minutes. Eligibility requires completion of 120 hours of physiotherapy instruction. May be taken alone or alongside other NBCE parts; state requirements for a passing PHT score vary. [NBCE, Physiotherapy Exam, https://www.mynbce.org/physiotherapy/, checked 2026-09-03]
  - Summary sequence: Part I mid-program (year 2) → Part II as clinical training begins → Part III and Part IV both cluster near the end of the program (Part III within 9 months of graduation; Part IV within 6 months of graduation) → PHT any time after 120 hours of coursework. [Synthesized from NBCE pages above and Wikipedia, National Board of Chiropractic Examiners, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Board_of_Chiropractic_Examiners, checked 2026-09-03]

- **State licensure variation:** All states require NBCE Parts I–IV (Wikipedia states "all but one" state has adopted NBCE Parts I–IV as of its last update — treat the "one holdout" detail as **NOT VERIFIED** for the current year and confirm against the specific state boards involved before publishing). Beyond NBCE exams, each state chiropractic board sets its own additional licensure requirements and scope-of-practice rules, which vary substantially. [Wikipedia, National Board of Chiropractic Examiners, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Concrete scope-of-practice examples, from a cross-sectional survey of state jurisdictions:**
  - Broadest scopes reported: Missouri, New Mexico, Kansas, Utah, Oklahoma, Illinois, Alabama.
  - Most restrictive scopes reported: New Hampshire, Hawaii, Michigan, New Jersey, Mississippi, Texas.
  - 90% or more of jurisdictions surveyed do **not** include prescription rights, obstetric care, or minor surgery within chiropractic scope.
  - Needling: Texas permits needle use only to draw blood for diagnostic purposes and for acupuncture; other states set different rules for dry needling and needle EMG.
  - Advanced imaging (CT/MRI ordering or performing) is allowed in some jurisdictions (e.g., Connecticut); Ohio requires diplomate status from the American Chiropractic Board of Radiology for some advanced imaging privileges.
  - [Chiro.org summary of "The Chiropractic Scope of Practice in the United States: A Cross-Sectional Survey," https://chiro.org/LINKS/ABSTRACTS/The_Chiropractic_Scope_of_Practice.shtml, checked 2026-09-03 — this is a secondary summary of a published survey; verify the underlying primary survey (state count and year of data collection) before quoting specific rankings in the book, as scope changes yearly and the source publication date was not independently confirmed in this pass. **NOT VERIFIED**: exact publication year of the underlying survey.]
  - **New Mexico** is the clearest, independently verifiable example of an expanded-scope state: a "certified advanced practice chiropractic physician" credential (created by state statute) authorizes prescribing, administering, and dispensing a defined formulary — herbal and homeopathic medicines, OTC drugs, vitamins/minerals/enzymes, glandular products, amino acids, dietary supplements, bioidentical hormones, sterile water/saline, Sarapin, caffeine, procaine, oxygen, epinephrine, and vapocoolants — with dangerous drugs, controlled substances, and injectable drugs requiring separate approval from the state pharmacy and medical boards. This statute is currently scheduled to sunset (repeal effective July 1, 2028). [New Mexico Statutes, Chapter 61, Article 4, Section 61-4-9.2, https://law.justia.com/codes/new-mexico/chapter-61/article-4/section-61-4-9-2/, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Cost and debt:** A 2024/2025 survey study of US chiropractors found mean student loan debt at graduation of **$176,297** (median $185,000; IQR $120,000–$240,000). Mean estimated total cost of chiropractic education (based on published cost-of-attendance data from 13 chiropractic programs) was **$260,642** (median $277,452; IQR $245,304–$280,050). The same study found graduates tended to underestimate their own eventual debt by roughly $50,000 relative to institutional cost-of-attendance figures. [Survey study, PLOS ONE, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0347127, checked 2026-09-03] Some chiropractic programs rank among graduate programs nationally with the highest debt-to-earnings ratios. [Wikipedia, Chiropractic education, citing 2024 data, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Number of accredited programs:** Counts vary by source and by whether Canadian/Puerto Rican programs are included. One CCE-linked directory listed roughly 18–20 CCE-accredited DC programs based in the United States; ACA states "19 nationally (U.S.) accredited, chiropractic doctoral graduate education programs across the United States, Canada and Puerto Rico." Treat the precise figure as approximate (18–20 US-based programs) rather than a fixed number, since new programs have opened and at least one (Palmer College of Chiropractic West) closed in 2025. [CCE program directory summary, https://www.cce-usa.org/doctor-of-chiropractic-programs.html, checked 2026-09-03; FCLB college directory, https://fclb.org/chiropractic-colleges/United+States, checked 2026-09-03; ACA Key Facts, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Number of practicing chiropractors:** BLS (2025 estimate) reports approximately **61,700 chiropractors employed** in the US, with 9% projected employment growth 2025–2035. ACA states "about 70,000 chiropractors" plus roughly 3,000 in academic/management roles — the ACA figure appears to include non-clinically-active licensees or a broader estimate than the BLS wage-and-salary/self-employed figure. Treat 60,000–70,000 as the defensible range and cite whichever source frames the claim (BLS = federal employment statistics methodology; ACA = professional association estimate). [BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, checked 2026-09-03; ACA Key Facts, checked 2026-09-03]

## 2. Clinical Evidence

- **ACP 2017 low back pain guideline** ("Noninvasive Treatments for Acute, Subacute, and Chronic Low Back Pain," Annals of Internal Medicine, Qaseem et al.):
  - For **acute/subacute** low back pain, the guideline places spinal manipulation alongside superficial heat, massage, and acupuncture as first-line options, stating clinicians and patients should select a nonpharmacologic treatment because "spinal manipulation are all appropriate treatment options to try initially" (most patients recover regardless of treatment). Evidence quality for manipulation specifically here is rated **low**: "Based on evidence of low quality, spinal manipulation results in minimal improvement in function compared with sham manipulation; data were insufficient to make conclusions about how it affects pain." [AAFP summary of ACP guideline, https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2017/0915/p407.html, checked 2026-09-03]
  - For **chronic** low back pain, the guideline recommends starting with nonpharmacologic treatment including exercise, multidisciplinary rehabilitation, acupuncture, mindfulness-based stress reduction, tai chi, yoga, motor control exercise, progressive relaxation, EMG biofeedback, low-level laser therapy, operant therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy, **or spinal manipulation** — these are presented as first-line options with fewer harms than medication. Evidence quality against sham therapy: "Compared with sham therapies...spinal manipulation does not result in a difference in pain." [Same source, checked 2026-09-03]
  - The guideline does **not** single out spinal manipulation, or chiropractic specifically, as a preferred or superior first-line option — it appears in a list of several nonpharmacologic options of similarly modest, low-quality evidence. Book authors should not overstate the guideline as an "endorsement" of chiropractic specifically.

- **Cochrane review, acute low back pain (Rubinstein et al., update):** Reviewed 20 RCTs, 2,674 participants. Conclusion, quoted: **"SMT is no more effective in participants with acute low-back pain than inert interventions, sham SMT, or when added to another intervention."** The review notes decisions about referral should weigh cost, patient/provider preference, and relative safety versus other options, and flags its own limitation of small numbers of studies per comparison. [Cochrane, https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD008880_spinal-manipulative-therapy-acute-low-back-pain, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Cochrane review, chronic low back pain (de Zoete et al., update CD008112.pub3):** Found spinal manipulative therapy (SMT) "may result in a small improvement in pain and medium improvement in functional status" versus sham; "medium improvement in pain and large improvement in functional status" versus no treatment; and "little to no difference in pain and small improvement in functional status" versus other conservative interventions. Certainty of evidence was rated **low to very low**, driven by heterogeneity in SMT technique, dosage, and frequency across trials. No serious adverse effects were found; minor transient soreness was occasionally reported. [Cochrane, https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD008112_spinal-manipulative-therapy-chronic-low-back-pain, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Neck pain (Cochrane, Gross et al., manipulation/mobilisation for mechanical neck disorders):** Manipulation or mobilization used **alone** showed no benefit in pain relief compared with placebo, control, or other treatments across single or multiple (3–11) session courses. When manipulation/mobilization was **combined with exercise**, the evidence showed measurable benefit for pain, function, and overall perceived effect in persistent neck disorders. Manipulation and mobilization were not shown to differ from each other in effectiveness. Insufficient evidence existed to draw conclusions for neck pain with radicular symptoms. [Cochrane-affiliated summary, https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-cochrane-review-of-manipulation-and-mobilization-for-mechanical/, checked 2026-09-03 — note this source describes an earlier iteration of the review (33 RCTs); the most current Cochrane update is CD004249.pub4 (Gross 2015), which could not be directly fetched in this pass due to a server error. **NOT VERIFIED**: whether the pub4 (2015) update materially changed these conclusions — recommend authors confirm directly against CD004249.pub4 before publication.]

- **Cervicogenic headache (Fernandez et al., 2020 systematic review/meta-analysis, 7 RCTs):** Spinal manipulative therapy showed small, statistically significant short-term improvements versus other manual therapies in pain intensity, pain frequency, and disability, but no effect on pain duration. At intermediate and long-term follow-up, no significant effects were found for pain intensity or frequency. Authors describe SMT as an effective short-term option while noting high-quality evidence is lacking. [PubMed abstract summary, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32621321/, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Migraine (2024 updated systematic review and meta-analysis, 6 RCTs, 645 patients):** Spinal manipulation showed **no effect** on migraine intensity/severity (SMD −0.22, 95% CI −0.65 to 0.21), no meaningful effect on duration (SMD −0.10, 95% CI −0.33 to 0.12) or emotional quality of life (SMD −14.47, 95% CI −31.59 to 2.66) — all rated **very low certainty**. Adverse events were **more common** with SMT than controls: risk ratio 2.06 (95% CI 1.24–3.41), number needed to harm = 6 (this NNH is for adverse events generally, not specifically serious vascular events — the review's discussion separately raises the theoretical concern that "even the slightest risk of carotid or vertebral artery dissections, strokes or deaths inevitably shifts the risk-benefit ratio towards the negative," but the NNH=6 figure itself reflects the pooled adverse-event rate across trials, which in these trials were predominantly mild/transient). Authors' quoted conclusion: **"The effectiveness of SMT for the treatment of migraines remains unproven"** and "SMT cannot be considered an evidence-based therapy for this condition." [PMC, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11606176/, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Non-musculoskeletal conditions — infantile colic:** Cochrane review (Dobson et al., manipulative therapies for infantile colic) found studies were "generally small and methodologically prone to bias," making a definitive effectiveness conclusion impossible. Critically, **unblinded parent-reported outcomes** (5 of 6 trials) suggested a statistically significant reduction in crying of roughly 1 hour 12 minutes/day, but when restricted to trials with **low risk of performance bias (blinded observers)**, the effect **did not reach statistical significance**. Adverse-event data were reported in only one of six trials, so safety conclusions could not be drawn. [Cochrane, https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD004796_manipulative-therapies-infantile-colic, checked 2026-09-03]

- **CONTESTED — colic, industry framing vs. Cochrane:** The American Chiropractic Association's public statement cites a single 2012 study reporting a 48% decrease in daily crying time for chiropractic-treated colicky infants versus 18% for untreated infants — this is a positive framing drawn from parent-reported data of exactly the kind the Cochrane review found unreliable once blinding is accounted for. Authors should present both: the ACA's cited figure, and the Cochrane finding that blinded-observer data did not confirm a significant effect. [ACA Statement on Pediatric Chiropractic Care, https://www.acatoday.org/news-publications/aca-statement-on-pediatric-chiropractic-care/, checked 2026-09-03; Cochrane CD004796, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Otitis media, asthma, other pediatric non-MSK conditions:** A 2007 systematic review (Ernst, "Chiropractic care for nonmusculoskeletal conditions") concluded there is "little data to suggest improvement of otitis media, asthma, nocturnal enuresis or attention deficit hyperactivity disorder." A later pediatric-focused review found chiropractic literature on acute otitis media relies primarily on case reports/case series, with "no credible solid evidence upon which to make recommendations," and separately noted chiropractors typically lack training in otoscopy sufficient to reliably diagnose middle-ear disease themselves. For asthma, manipulation was associated with subjective symptom improvement in some studies but "little impact on objective markers of the disease" — a distinction the review attributes to possible effects on thoracic-cage mobility rather than any effect on airway obstruction pathology. A 1998 randomized trial in the New England Journal of Medicine found chiropractic spinal manipulation provided "no benefit" for pediatric asthma beyond usual care. [PubMed abstract summary, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17604553/, checked 2026-09-03 (content largely inaccessible directly — summary triangulated via secondary source); Springer, Chiropractic & Manual Therapies, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1746-1340-18-14, checked 2026-09-03; Quackwatch summary citing the 1998 NEJM asthma RCT, https://quackwatch.org/chiropractic/dd/ped/, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Hypertension:** No high-quality trial evidence supports spinal manipulation as an effective hypertension treatment. This section of research is thin; the specific 2012 "Spinal Manipulation for the Treatment of Hypertension: A Systematic Qualitative Literature Review" could not be directly fetched and read in full during this pass (PubMed access blocked). **NOT VERIFIED**: the precise wording of that review's conclusion. Authors should independently pull the full text (Goertz et al., Journal of Manipulative and Physiological Therapeutics, 2012) before citing a specific verdict, rather than relying on this file. What can be stated with confidence: hypertension is **not** an accepted primary indication for chiropractic care in any mainstream clinical guideline, and available trials are small, heterogeneous, and not sufficient to establish a clinically meaningful blood-pressure-lowering effect. [Search triangulation only, checked 2026-09-03 — flagged NOT VERIFIED for exact review conclusion wording]

- **Adverse events and cervical artery dissection — general risk framing:**
  - A UK prospective national survey (Thiel et al.) of 28,807 treatment consultations and 50,276 cervical manipulations across 19,722 patients recorded **zero serious adverse events**; using Hanley's "rule of three" to estimate an upper-bound 95% CI on a zero-event dataset, the study estimated a risk of approximately **1 per 10,000 treatment consultations** for a serious adverse event immediately post-treatment, **2 per 10,000 consultations** within 7 days, or about **6 per 100,000 individual cervical manipulations**. Authors characterized this as "low to very low" risk. [Chiro.org summary of Thiel et al., https://chiro.org/Stroke/Safety_of_Chiropractic_Manipulation.shtml, checked 2026-09-03 — note: this is a zero-event upper-bound statistical estimate, not an observed event rate; and it reflects UK registered-chiropractor practice, which may not generalize directly to all US settings.]
  - A 2012 meta-analysis (Haynes et al., cited via ACA) found risk of vertebral artery dissection following manipulation "cannot be ruled out," and separately noted possible underestimation of true risk due to no mandatory adverse-event reporting requirement for private chiropractic practice. [ACA, Vertebral Artery Dissection: An Evidence Review, https://www.acatoday.org/news-publications/vertebral-artery-dissection-an-evidence-review/, checked 2026-09-03 — note this is a professional-association-published summary; treat its framing as advocacy-adjacent even where the underlying facts are accurate.]

- **Cassidy 2008 case-crossover study (Ontario population-based case-control/case-crossover study of DC visits, PCP visits, and VBA stroke):**
  - Design: compared rates of chiropractic (DC) and primary-care-physician (PCP) visits in the days/weeks preceding vertebrobasilar artery (VBA) stroke, using both case-control and case-crossover designs.
  - Finding, quoted: **"We found no evidence of excess risk of VBA stroke associated with chiropractic care compared to primary care."** Both DC and PCP visits showed elevated association with subsequent VBA stroke at similar magnitudes.
  - Authors' interpretation: the elevated risk after **both** types of visit is "likely due to patients with headache and neck pain from VBA dissection seeking care before their stroke" — i.e., patients with an early, already-occurring dissection seek any kind of care (chiropractic or medical) for the resulting head/neck pain, and the stroke that follows is a manifestation of the pre-existing dissection rather than something the visit (of either kind) caused.
  - What the study explicitly could **not** rule out: the authors themselves stated they could "not rule out neck manipulation as a potential cause of some VBA strokes" — e.g., manipulation dislodging a pre-existing thrombus. A design limitation raised in later commentary: the 0–1 day PCP cohort was excluded from the published comparison, which limits the ability to test same-day causation directly against a same-day PCP baseline.
  - Overall: this is a well-known and heavily cited study, but it establishes an **association pattern consistent with reverse causation** (pre-existing dissection driving care-seeking) rather than either proving or fully disproving that manipulation itself can, in some rare cases, precipitate dissection/stroke. Later population studies (Cassidy et al. 2017, Whedon et al. 2015) are reported to show broadly consistent patterns. [Secondary summary, https://www.thechiropracticexperts.com/a-commentary-on-cassidy-2008/, checked 2026-09-03; corroborated in general shape by ACA evidence review above — **NOT VERIFIED directly against the original Spine 2008 Cassidy et al. paper in this pass**; authors should pull the primary paper (Cassidy JD et al., Spine 2008;33(4 Suppl):S176-83) before quoting numeric odds ratios, as this file only verified the qualitative conclusion and stated limitation, not the specific odds ratios.]
  - A separate biomechanical study (Gorrell et al., cited via ACA) measured vertebral artery length change during simulated manipulation: less than 5% elongation on the non-thrust side, versus roughly 33.5% elongation needed to take up normal arterial slack and roughly 51% elongation associated with mechanical injury in prior testing — suggesting standard manipulative technique does not mechanically stretch the artery far enough to explain injury by simple stretch mechanism. [ACA Vertebral Artery Dissection evidence review, checked 2026-09-03 — **NOT VERIFIED** against the Gorrell primary paper directly.]
  - Bottom line for the book: state plainly that (a) cervical artery dissection is a rare, serious condition that can occur spontaneously or after trivial neck movement of any kind, (b) case-crossover/case-control epidemiology has been unable to demonstrate that chiropractic manipulation increases VBA stroke risk beyond the risk associated with visiting a PCP for the same early symptoms, (c) this is widely interpreted as evidence **against** manipulation being a major independent cause, but (d) it does not amount to proof of zero causal risk in rare individual cases, and prospective consent discussions in practice should say so.

- **Red flags requiring referral rather than manipulation**, per an international framework review of low-back-pain guidelines, in four categories:
  - **Malignancy:** history of cancer, unexplained/unintentional weight loss (most consistently endorsed across guidelines); also pain at rest, pain at night, elevated inflammatory markers, loss of appetite.
  - **Fracture:** major/significant trauma, use of steroids or immunosuppressants, age over 50, female sex (osteoporotic fracture risk).
  - **Infection:** fever, intravenous drug use, corticosteroid/immunosuppressant therapy, night pain, bone tenderness over lumbar spinous processes.
  - **Cauda equina syndrome:** saddle anesthesia (perineal numbness) and (sudden-onset) bladder dysfunction were the two most consistently cited signs across guidelines (present in 9 of the guidelines reviewed).
  - The review's own critical finding: substantial variation exists across guidelines about which red flags to prioritize, and the diagnostic accuracy evidence behind many individual red flags is limited. [Springer, European Spine Journal review, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00586-016-4684-0, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Pediatric and perinatal chiropractic — evidence base and consent standard (present neutrally, both positions):**
  - **Professional-association position (ACA):** states pediatric chiropractic care, "when administered properly, is effective, safe and gentle," citing a 2014 literature review spanning over a century of practice concluding serious adverse events in infants/children are "exceedingly rare," with no deaths reported in the academic literature to date, and stating chiropractors treating children pay the same malpractice insurance rates as those treating adults only. [ACA Statement on Pediatric Chiropractic Care, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **ICPA (International Chiropractic Pediatric Association) consent position:** states it "respects each individual's right to make informed, conscious health care choices," framed with reference to general medical-ethics informed-consent principles (patient understanding of condition and available treatments). [ICPA Positional Statement on Informed Consent, https://icpa4kids.com/about/positional-statements/informed-consent/, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **Independent/critical literature:** A 2010 review states "the efficacy of chiropractic care in the treatment of non-musculoskeletal disorders has yet to be definitively proven or disproven" in children, and separately criticizes some practitioner claims about pediatric non-MSK benefit as unsupported, at times to the point of overstatement. [Springer, Chiropractic & Manual Therapies, checked 2026-09-03] A Quackwatch-published critique cites a systematic review identifying **14 manipulation-related pediatric injuries** (ages up to 18) including 9 serious cases and 2 deaths (brain hemorrhage; atlas dislocation), and cites two academic-chiropractor authors stating pediatric health claims "continue to be supported by only low levels of scientific evidence." [Quackwatch, "Be Wary of Chiropractic Pediatrics," https://quackwatch.org/chiropractic/dd/ped/, checked 2026-09-03 — this source is explicitly critical/advocacy in the opposite direction from ACA/ICPA; present as one pole of a CONTESTED debate, and independently verify the "14 injuries / 9 serious / 2 deaths" figure against its named primary systematic review before quoting it as a headline statistic, since it was only confirmed here via a secondary summary. **NOT VERIFIED** at primary-source level within this research pass.]
  - **What can be stated as settled:** high-quality randomized evidence for chiropractic treatment of non-musculoskeletal pediatric conditions (colic, otitis media, asthma, ADHD, nocturnal enuresis) is lacking or inconsistent per Cochrane and independent systematic reviews above; serious adverse events from pediatric manipulation appear to be rare in the literature that exists, but that literature itself is thin (mostly case reports/series, without a mandatory national adverse-event reporting system), so absence of documented events is not equivalent to demonstrated safety at a population level. This is a genuinely contested area between professional-association and independent-academic framing, and chapter authors should present it as such rather than adopt either side's framing wholesale.

## 3. Technique

- **Diversified Technique:** The most widely used chiropractic technique (reported at roughly 96% of chiropractors using it in some form), non-proprietary and eclectic. Assessment is via palpation (static and motion) plus patient history/orthopedic-neurologic exam. Correction is delivered as a high-velocity, low-amplitude (HVLA) manual thrust aimed at a specific spinal segment to restore joint motion. [Wikipedia, Chiropractic treatment techniques, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiropractic_treatment_techniques, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Gonstead Technique:** Developed in 1923 (Clarence Gonstead). Distinguished by removing rotational component from the thrust and by extensive use of specific instrumentation for assessment: full-spine X-ray analysis, the Gonstead Radiographic Parallel measuring system, and the Nervo-Scope, a handheld device measuring paraspinal skin temperature differentials claimed to indicate subluxation/nerve interference. Correction is a hands-on, segment-specific HVLA thrust guided by this analysis. [Wikipedia, Chiropractic treatment techniques, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Activator Methods Chiropractic Technique (AMCT):** A mechanical-force, manually-assisted (MFMA) technique developed by Arlan Fuhr, using a spring-loaded handheld instrument rather than a manual thrust. Assessment involves the patient prone, comparing functional leg lengths and using manual muscle testing/isolation tests to localize the segment to be treated. The Activator instrument delivers a small, controlled impulse (no more than approximately 0.3 joules over a roughly 3-millisecond pulse) to the target segment. [Wikipedia, Chiropractic treatment techniques, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Thompson (Drop-Table) Technique:** Uses a segmented adjusting table with sections that drop slightly under the practitioner's controlled HVLA thrust, adding gravitational assistance to the correction and reducing the force needed from the practitioner. [Ground Chiropractic technique summary, https://groundchiro.co.uk/8-chiropractic-techniques-explained/, checked 2026-09-03 — a practice-website secondary source rather than an academic one; core mechanical description is consistent with widely reported descriptions of the technique but treat any additional specific claims from this source with caution.]

- **Sacro Occipital Technique (SOT):** Founded on the premise that the relationship between pelvis, spine, and cranium (sacrum and occiput specifically) governs overall spinal function, and that correcting pelvic positioning has downstream effects on the rest of the spine. Correction typically uses graduated wedge-shaped blocks placed under the pelvis, with the patient's own body weight and breathing used to apply gentle, sustained corrective force rather than a discrete HVLA thrust. [Ground Chiropractic technique summary, checked 2026-09-03 — same caveat as above.]

- **Flexion Distraction (Cox Technique):** Developed for disc-related conditions. Uses a specialized segmented table that provides rhythmic, gentle flexion and traction/distraction forces to the lumbar spine rather than a thrust, intended to reduce intradiscal pressure and nerve root compression. [Ground Chiropractic technique summary, checked 2026-09-03; general mechanism consistent with widely reported descriptions.]

- **Applied Kinesiology (AK):** Developed by George Goodheart (chiropractor), 1964; the International College of Applied Kinesiology was formed 1973–1976. Assessment is via manual muscle testing: the practitioner applies resistance to a target muscle and evaluates the quality/smoothness of the muscle's response (not raw strength) as a marker claimed to reflect organ dysfunction, structural imbalance, or other conditions via a "viscerosomatic relationship" concept; variants include nutrient/substance testing and "therapy localization." Treatment guided by AK findings may include manipulation, myofascial work, or nutritional recommendations. Roughly 37.6% of US chiropractors reported using AK as of a 2003 survey. **Scientific evidence is strongly negative**: controlled studies (e.g., a 1988 study) found AK testing performed no better than random guessing, and reviews of test-retest reliability, inter-tester reliability, and diagnostic accuracy found results consistent with chance. The American Cancer Society states scientific evidence does not support AK for diagnosing or treating cancer or other illness. [Wikipedia, Applied kinesiology, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_kinesiology, checked 2026-09-03]

- **NUCCA (National Upper Cervical Chiropractic Association) technique:** Founded 1966 by Ralph Gregory, building on earlier work with John Grostic. Uses precise X-ray imaging of the head and neck with "double-pivot-point" mathematical analysis to calculate an individualized misalignment pattern, plus posture-measurement instrumentation (an "Anatometer") to track ongoing status with fewer repeat X-rays. Correction is described as gentle and non-thrust, non-twisting, delivered as a specific, low-force, precisely vectored contact at the upper cervical spine (occiput/atlas region) rather than a traditional HVLA "pop." [Wikipedia, NUCCA, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NUCCA, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Blair Technique:** Developed by William G. Blair (Palmer graduate), practicing from 1949, teaching the technique from around 1961. Distinguished by highly individualized, non-orthogonal X-ray analysis of the atlanto-occipital joint (a specialized "Blair Cervical Series" of customized angled views using a Blair Protractoclamp positioning device), plus paraspinal dermothermography and functional leg-length assessment for ongoing monitoring. Correction ("Blair Toggle-Torque") uses a toggle mechanism without recoil, incorporating roughly 180-degree torque and a sustained "pisiform lead" contact point through the thrust, rather than the quick recoil motion used in some other upper-cervical techniques. The source describing this technique calls it the only non-orthogonal precision spinographic/adjustive cervical technique, distinguishing it from orthogonally-based methods (including NUCCA and Toggle Recoil) that trace to B.J. Palmer/Grostic. [Chiro.org summary, https://chiro.org/LINKS/ABSTRACTS/blair.shtml, checked 2026-09-03 — a chiropractic-community source; core historical/mechanical claims are internally consistent with independent descriptions of the technique but not cross-verified against an independent academic source in this pass.]

- **Toggle Recoil (Hole-In-One / HIO, associated with B.J. Palmer):** A historically foundational upper-cervical technique using a specific, quick "toggle" hand contact against the atlas followed by rapid withdrawal ("recoil"), typically with the patient in a knee-chest position. **NOT VERIFIED IN DETAIL**: attempts to fetch a substantive independent description of assessment protocol and mechanics in this research pass returned only navigational/directory content; authors should independently source a fuller technical description (e.g., from Palmer College archival/technique literature) before writing detailed technique-chapter content on Toggle Recoil specifically.

- **HVLA thrust mechanics and the cavitation sound:** A high-velocity, low-amplitude (HVLA) thrust is a rapid (high-velocity), small-displacement (low-amplitude) manual force applied to move a spinal (or other) joint briefly past its passive range of motion, typically producing an audible "crack" or "pop" in many but not all cases. The 2015 real-time MRI study (Kawchuk et al., PLOS ONE) directly visualized this event and found that joint cracking results from **cavity inception within the synovial fluid** — i.e., a new gas cavity forming as the joint surfaces are rapidly separated (a process termed **tribonucleation**) — rather than the previously dominant theory that the sound came from the **collapse** of a pre-existing gas bubble. Quoted finding: **"joint cracking is the result of cavity inception within synovial fluid rather than collapse of a pre-existing bubble."** [PLOS ONE, Kawchuk et al. 2015, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0119470, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Reliability of common assessment methods** (from a systematic review of methods used to locate manipulable spinal lesions, and a related systematic review of manual palpation reliability/validity for low back pain, both giving kappa (κ) values):
  - **Pain provocation on static/motion palpation:** generally fair to good reliability — lumbar facet joint pain on static palpation κ = 0.38–0.73; lumbar spinous process pain κ = 0.21–0.57; sacroiliac joint pain κ = 0.14–0.59; prone instability test (muscle-contraction phase) κ = 0.46–0.87 (relaxation phase lower, κ = 0.30–0.41; combined score κ = 0.10); soft tissue tenderness reliability inconsistent (sciatic nerve pain κ = 0.80 in one study, gluteal tender points κ = 0.51–0.68, lumbar paraspinal muscle pain κ = 0.34 in others).
  - **Segmental mobility/motion assessment (i.e., detecting which segment is "stuck" or hypomobile), rather than pain provocation:** substantially **worse** reliability — static palpation for segmental mobility at lumbar facet joints κ = −0.17 to 0.17, lumbar spinous processes κ = −0.02 to 0.26, sacroiliac joints κ = −0.11 to −0.10 (i.e., at or below chance). Motion palpation (dynamic testing) of sacroiliac joint motion fared somewhat better: κ = 0.14–0.75 inter-rater, κ = 0.23–0.73 intra-rater; sacral base position on flexion κ = 0.37, on extension κ = 0.05.
  - **Overall conclusion of the review:** "reliability of soft tissue structures palpation is inconsistent, and reliability of bony structures and joint mobility palpation is poor." In short: chiropractors and other manual therapists can reasonably reliably agree on **where a patient reports pain when pressed**, but agree poorly on **which specific segment is hypomobile or "restricted"** — the core diagnostic construct much manual technique selection is nominally based on. [Systematic review, PMC, https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8390263, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **Leg length inequality assessment:** A related review found "high quality evidence supporting the use, with limitations, of static and motion palpation, and measures of leg length inequality" as clinically applied tests, though it did not isolate specific kappa figures in the portion reviewed. [Chiropractic & Manual Therapies review, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2045-709X-21-36, checked 2026-09-03 — treat as a more favorable secondary framing than the pure-reliability review above; the two should be read together rather than either alone.]
  - **Thermography (skin temperature differential measurement, e.g., as used in Gonstead's Nervo-Scope and some upper-cervical protocols):** the same review found indirect assessment methods "such as skin conductance or thermography — tend not to be supported by the available evidence," concluding the evidence was **unfavourable** for these indirect methods relative to direct palpation methods. [Same source, checked 2026-09-03]

- **The 1895 Harvey Lillard account:**
  - **Palmer's own account** (per his 1910 book): Palmer stated the manipulation of Lillard was deliberate, purposeful, and specific — "there was nothing accidental about this, as it was accomplished with an object in view," further claiming the adjustment was so specific "no chiropractor has equaled it." Palmer's account describes finding a "lump" between Lillard's shoulders and manipulating it, curing what Palmer believed was nerve-impingement-caused deafness.
  - **Documentary record — inconsistent dating:** the exact date is not settled across Palmer's own retellings and secondary sources — cited dates range across September 6, September 15, September 18 (1895 most commonly cited), with some sources giving alternative months (January–April) and even alternative years (1894–1896). There is evidence B.J. Palmer may have altered details (month/year) in later retellings, and B.J. Palmer separately gave sworn testimony describing a different vertebra being adjusted (the axis) than in other tellings (which describe a thoracic/shoulder-blade-area adjustment) — internally inconsistent across the Palmer family's own accounts.
  - **Lillard's own 1897 published account** (in *The Chiropractic*, a period publication): quoted as saying, **"Last January Dr. Palmer told me that my deafness came from an injury in my spine... Dr. Palmer treated me on the spine; in two treatments I could hear quite well. That was eight months ago."** This places initial treatment around **January–May 1895**, not September — a direct contradiction with the commonly cited September 1895 date.
  - **Lillard's daughter's later account** (Valdeenia Lillard Simons, as reported): describes the event very differently — that Lillard was not receiving treatment at all, but was **telling a joke in the hallway** outside Palmer's office; that Palmer, laughing at the punchline, slapped Lillard on the back with the hand holding a heavy book he'd been reading; that Lillard mentioned improved hearing some days later; and that Palmer only then began exploring manipulation as a business opportunity. Simons's account also describes a profit-sharing agreement with her father that she says was never honored. This directly conflicts with B.J. Palmer's later sworn claim that Lillard had been "thoroughly deaf" — Simons's account implies Lillard could hear well enough to be actively engaged in telling a joke to a friend just before the incident.
  - **Assessment for the book:** treat the canonical "first chiropractic adjustment cured deafness" narrative as a **founding origin story with a contested, internally inconsistent documentary record**, not as settled history. Present Palmer's account, Lillard's own 1897 quote, and the daughter's later account side by side; do not adjudicate which is "true." [Wikipedia, William Harvey Lillard, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Harvey_Lillard, checked 2026-09-03; Wikipedia, Chiropractic controversy and criticism, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiropractic_controversy_and_criticism, checked 2026-09-03; Science-Based Medicine, "Chiropractic and Deafness: Back to 1895," https://sciencebasedmedicine.org/chiropractic-and-deafness-back-to-1895/, checked 2026-09-03 — note this last source describes a widow's later comment that Lillard remained deaf until his death as an explicitly labeled **unconfirmed rumor**; do not present it as fact.]

- **Wilk v. American Medical Association:**
  - **Filed: 1976** (Chester Wilk, DC, and other chiropractor plaintiffs, against the AMA and co-defendant medical organizations). [Wikipedia, Wilk v. American Medical Association, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilk_v._American_Medical_Association, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **AMA Committee on Quackery:** formed **November 1963** under the AMA's Department of Investigation; per Judge Getzendanner's 1987 opinion, its explicit purpose was described in Wilk's argument (accepted by the court) as intended to "contain and eliminate the chiropractic profession." The Committee on Quackery itself **disbanded in December 1974**; the parent AMA Department of Investigation was disbanded in **May 1975**, at which point Doyl Taylor (who had led the anti-chiropractic effort) left AMA employment. [Justia, Wilk v. American Medical Ass'n, 671 F. Supp. 1465 (N.D. Ill. 1987), https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/671/1465/2595129/, checked 2026-09-03] — this confirms the "1963 to 1975" span given in the research brief as accurate: 1963 formation of the Committee on Quackery to 1975 disbandment of its parent department (the Committee itself formally ended in Dec. 1974, with the broader department closing in May 1975).
  - **Principle 3 of the AMA's (pre-1980) Principles of Medical Ethics**, quoted: **"A physician should practice a method of healing founded on a scientific basis; and he should not voluntarily professionally associate with anyone who violates this principle."** This principle was used as the ethical basis for AMA members being told association with chiropractors was unethical. It was **removed from the AMA ethics code in 1980**. [Wikipedia, Wilk v. American Medical Association, checked 2026-09-03; corroborated by Justia opinion text, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **1987 Getzendanner decision — quoted finding:** **"The AMA and its officials instituted a boycott of chiropractors in the mid-1960s by informing AMA members that chiropractors were unscientific practitioners and that it was unethical for a medical physician to associate with chiropractors. The purpose of the boycott was to contain and eliminate the chiropractic profession."** The court found the AMA had engaged in an unlawful conspiracy in restraint of trade in violation of Section 1 of the Sherman Antitrust Act, running from 1966 to at least 1980 (when Principle 3 was removed), and found the AMA had not met its burden of proving the boycott was a legitimate "patient care" measure — the court held a boycott was unnecessarily broad compared to less restrictive alternatives such as public education.
  - **Remedy ordered:** a **permanent injunction** against the AMA under Section 16 of the Clayton Act, barring similar future restraint-of-trade conduct against chiropractors, on the grounds that lingering effects of the conspiracy persisted, the AMA had never publicly acknowledged the unlawful conduct, and no affirmative corrective statement had been issued to members. The court explicitly declined to grant the broadest injunctive relief plaintiffs sought, but stated "a proper exercise of the court's discretion permits, and in my judgment requires, an injunction." The Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the judgment in 1990. [Justia, Wilk v. American Medical Ass'n opinion text, checked 2026-09-03; Wikipedia, Wilk v. American Medical Association, checked 2026-09-03]

## 5. Anatomy

- **Vertebral counts by region (standard):** Cervical **7** (C1–C7), Thoracic **12** (T1–T12), Lumbar **5** (L1–L5), Sacral **5** (fused into one sacrum), Coccygeal **~4** (fused, variable, forming the coccyx). Total pre-fusion is 33 vertebrae in early development, reducing functionally to 24 discrete (mobile) vertebrae plus the fused sacrum and coccyx in the adult. Sacral and coccygeal fusion begins around age 20 and is not complete until middle age. Nearly all mammals, regardless of body size, have 7 cervical vertebrae — this count is a fixed mammalian trait, not something that varies with body size. [OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology 2e, https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/7-3-the-vertebral-column, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Standard variations:** Lumbosacral transitional vertebrae (LSTV — sacralization of L5, or lumbarization of S1) occur with a reported prevalence ranging **4%–35.9%** across studies, with a commonly cited mean of roughly **12%**. Sacralization is reported more frequently in males; lumbarization more frequently in females. Most cases are incidental imaging findings without symptoms; only a subset are considered clinically significant. [Boneandspine.com clinical anatomy summary, https://boneandspine.com/lumbosacral-transitional-vertebra/, checked 2026-09-03 — a clinician-authored secondary reference rather than a single primary study; the wide 4%–36% range itself reflects genuine heterogeneity across the underlying primary literature, not source unreliability.]

- **Standard palpable surface landmarks** (verified against an anatomy/surface-landmark reference; two independent sources cross-checked and are consistent):
  - **C2 spinous process:** the first readily palpable bony midline structure below the external occipital protuberance (EOP), at the base of the skull. [Basicmedicalkey.com surface anatomy reference, https://basicmedicalkey.com/surface-anatomy-of-the-back-and-vertebral-levels-of-clinically-important-structures/, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **C7 (vertebra prominens):** the most prominent palpable spinous process at the base of the neck, especially with the neck flexed; C7 is the most prominent spinous process overall in roughly 60–70% of people (note: in the remaining people, T1 or another nearby level can be equally or more prominent — this is a well-known clinical caveat, so C7 identification by palpation alone is not 100% reliable). [Same source, checked 2026-09-03; corroborated by second independent source, https://getoncourse.ai/notes/us-medical-pg/step-1/anatomy/surface-anatomy/back-surface-landmarks/, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **T3 spinous process:** lies in the same horizontal plane as the root (medial end) of the scapular spine. [Confirmed by both sources above, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **T7 spinous process:** lies in the same horizontal plane as the **inferior angle of the scapula** — but only with the patient **standing/upright with arms at sides**; the correspondence shifts (commonly cited as closer to T6) if the patient is prone or if arm position changes, since scapular position moves with arm/shoulder position. Authors should specify patient position when citing this landmark. [Basicmedicalkey.com, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **L4 spinous process / vertebral body:** lies approximately in the horizontal plane connecting the tops (superior margins) of the iliac crests (the "supracristal plane" or Tuffier's line), used clinically as a landmark for safe lumbar puncture (spinal cord typically ends around L1–L2 in adults, well above this level). Caveat: in roughly **20% of the population**, this plane instead corresponds to the **L5** spinous process rather than L4 — i.e., this landmark has a known, quantified error rate and should be taught with that caveat rather than as exact in all patients. [Basicmedicalkey.com, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **S2 spinous tubercle / PSIS:** the posterior superior iliac spines (PSIS), often visible as a pair of skin dimples roughly 3 cm lateral to the midline, lie at the level of the S2 spinous tubercle. [Both sources above, checked 2026-09-03, consistent]

- **Normal ranges of motion — CONTESTED-by-source-variability, not by controversy:** published normative ROM values vary meaningfully across studies and reference sources depending on methodology (goniometry vs. inclinometry vs. imaging), population age, and measurement technique. Two reputable but differing reference sets, both included so authors can choose consistently rather than mixing numbers from different systems within one chapter:
  - **Set A (Physiopedia, citing AAOS-style norms):** Cervical flexion 80–90°, extension 50–70°, lateral flexion 20–45° each side, rotation 70–90° each side. [Physiopedia, Cervical Spine Range of Motion, https://www.physio-pedia.com/Cervical_Spine_Range_of_Motion, checked 2026-09-03 — this source itself cautions that "these numbers vary between individuals and research studies" and that published norms "often don't reflect what clinicians observe in practice."]
  - **Set B (Anatomy Standard biomechanics reference, pooled in-vivo study means):** Cervical — flexion 64°, extension 63° (combined flex/ext 127°), lateral bending 49° each side, axial rotation 85° each side. Thoracic — flexion 26°, extension 22° (combined 48°), lateral bending 30° each side, axial rotation 47° each side. Lumbar — flexion 65°, extension 31° (combined 96°), lateral bending 30° each side, axial rotation 15.3° each side. [Anatomystandard.com, https://www.anatomystandard.com/biomechanics/spine/rom-of-spine.html, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **Recommendation for authors:** pick one reference system (Set B is sourced to pooled in-vivo study data across regions and is internally consistent across cervical/thoracic/lumbar, which Set A is not, since Set A only covers cervical) and cite it consistently throughout the book rather than blending numbers from different systems, since the two sets are not directly reconcilable at the individual-degree level.

### 6.1 Before 1895

- **Hippocratic writings.** The *Corpus Hippocraticum* is a collection of roughly 60 medical books
  associated with the lifetime of Hippocrates (c. 460–370 BC). Spinal treatment appears chiefly in
  *On Articulations* (*Peri Arthron*), with related material in *On Fractures*, *Mochlikon*, *On the
  Nature of Bones*, and *On Places in Man*. Devices described include the **Hippocratic ladder**
  (patient bound to a ladder upright or inverted and subjected to *succussion*, shaking, with body
  weight supplying traction), the **Hippocratic board**, and the **Hippocratic scamnum** (a pivoting
  board combining axial traction with focal manual pressure). The text directs that "the physician,
  or some person who is strong, and not uninstructed, should apply the palm of the hand to the hump,"
  and describes practitioners using the foot to apply "gentle pressure" to a spinal deformity.
  [Historical overview of spinal deformities in ancient Greece, PMC2654856,
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2654856/, checked 2026-09-03; primary text also at
  Internet Classics Archive, *On the Articulations*, https://classics.mit.edu/Hippocrates/artic.44.44.html]
  - Note for authors: these texts describe **reduction of deformity and dislocation**. They do not
    contain a general theory that spinal displacement causes systemic disease. Do not present them as
    an ancient endorsement of chiropractic theory.

- **Bonesetting.** Folk practitioners who performed joint manipulation, reduction of dislocations,
  and setting of fractures, and who were the principal providers of such treatment before chiropractic
  and osteopathy existed. The trade was overwhelmingly **transmitted within families** across
  generations rather than taught formally; most bonesetters were self taught. Documented bonesetting
  families in Britain include the Taylors of Whitworth, the Thomas family of Anglesey, and the Matthew
  family of the Midlands, several practising for over two centuries. Named individual practitioners
  include Sally Mapp (18th century England) and Charles Sweet (New England). Bone injury treatment is
  documented far earlier still: the Edwin Smith Papyrus (c. 1552 BC) records Egyptian management of
  bone injuries. Bonesetting continues today in parts of South America, Asia and Africa where
  orthopedic specialists are scarce. [Wikipedia, Bonesetter,
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonesetter, checked 2026-09-03]
  - The same source states that chiropractors, osteopaths and physical therapists **replaced**
    bonesetters as the primary providers of manual joint treatment, but establishes **no direct
    causal or lineal relationship** between bonesetting and the founding of either profession. Authors
    should describe bonesetting as the surrounding practice environment, not as chiropractic's parent.

- **Osteopathy and A.T. Still.** The practice of osteopathy began in the United States in **1874**.
  Still founded the **American School of Osteopathy on May 20, 1892, in Kirksville, Missouri**, with
  an initial class of twenty one students, and in the same year published *The Philosophy and
  Mechanical Principles of Osteopathy*. Still held that disease and physiologic dysfunction were
  "etiologically grounded in a disordered musculoskeletal system," so that by diagnosing and treating
  the musculoskeletal system a physician could treat a wide variety of diseases. The self healing
  premise is stated by his son as the view that the body "would operate smoothly into old age, if
  properly maintained and that every living organism possessed the ability to produce all the
  necessary chemicals and materials to cure itself of ailments." [Wikipedia, Osteopathy,
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osteopathy, checked 2026-09-03; Wikipedia, Andrew Taylor Still,
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Taylor_Still, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **NOT VERIFIED:** the widely circulated Still aphorism "the rule of the artery is supreme" could
    not be confirmed against a primary Still text or an independent scholarly source in this pass. It
    does **not** appear in the Wikipedia articles on Still or on osteopathy. Do not present it as a
    direct quotation. Still's routing of the mechanism through **circulation and blood supply** (as
    against Palmer's routing through **nerve transmission**) is well attested and may be stated in the
    author's own words; the aphorism itself should not be quoted.

### 6.2 D.D. Palmer, the name, and the school

- **Daniel David Palmer**, born **March 7, 1845**, died **October 20, 1913**, in Los Angeles,
  California, at age 68. He practiced **magnetic healing** from the mid 1880s, in Burlington and then
  Davenport, Iowa. Palmer's own statement of the start of that practice: **"In 1886 I began as a
  business. Although I practiced under the name of magnetic, I did not slap or rub, as others."**
  [Wikipedia, Daniel David Palmer, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_David_Palmer, checked
  2026-09-03]

- **The name.** **Rev. Samuel H. Weed** suggested the word "chiropractic," formed from the Greek
  *cheiro* (hand) and *praktikos* (doing, action). Palmer was using the term by **1896**. [Wikipedia,
  History of chiropractic, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_chiropractic, checked 2026-09-03;
  Wikipedia, Daniel David Palmer, checked 2026-09-03]

- **The school — CONTESTED (minor, date only).** The Wikipedia biography of D.D. Palmer states he
  founded the **Palmer School and Cure in Davenport in 1897**, later renamed Palmer College of
  Chiropractic. The Wikipedia article on the history of chiropractic states instead that he
  "established his chiropractic school in **1896**, two years after" the Lillard event. The two dates
  appear in sources of equal standing and were not reconciled in this pass. **NOT VERIFIED:** the
  exact founding year. Authors following the course brief should use **1897** for the school and
  **1896** for the name, and may note that the school's founding date is given inconsistently even
  within a single reference work. [Both Wikipedia articles above, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Prosecution and the transfer to B.J.** Palmer was arrested more than once for unlicensed
  practice and was **convicted in 1906** for professing to cure disease without a license. He sold
  the school to his son shortly after that prosecution. **B.J. Palmer assumed control of the Palmer
  School in 1906**, expanded enrollment to over 1,000 students by the early 1920s, and became known
  as the "Philosopher of Chiropractic." The relationship between father and son is described as
  "tenuous and often bitter," particularly after the sale. Chiropractors accumulated roughly
  **15,000 prosecutions for practicing medicine without a license in the profession's first 30
  years**. [Wikipedia, History of chiropractic, checked 2026-09-03; Wikipedia, Daniel David Palmer,
  checked 2026-09-03]
  - **NOT VERIFIED:** the 15,000 prosecutions figure was confirmed only within Wikipedia's history
    article and not traced to its underlying source in this pass. Treat as approximate and attribute.

- **Straights and mixers.** "Straights" relied solely on spinal adjustment and adhered to Palmer's
  vitalist framework; "mixers" combined adjustment with other treatments and sought integration with
  scientific medicine. B.J. Palmer championed the straight position. [Wikipedia, History of
  chiropractic, checked 2026-09-03; Wikipedia, Chiropractic,
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiropractic, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Religion.** Wikipedia's chiropractic article states that D.D. and B.J. Palmer "seriously
  considered declaring chiropractic a religion" and decided against it, and that D.D. Palmer claimed
  he "received chiropractic from the other world." [Wikipedia, Chiropractic, checked 2026-09-03]

### 6.3 Innate Intelligence in Palmer's own vocabulary

- **D.D. Palmer's own formulations (1910).** A peer reviewed comparative analysis in the *Journal of
  Contemporary Chiropractic* records Palmer defining Innate Intelligence as **"the Soul, Spirit, or
  Spark of Life"** which **"controls and directs all the vital functions,"** and states
  **"Innate is a segment of that Intelligence which fills the universe."** The same article reports
  Palmer describing the concept as arriving "like a flash of light" through spiritual insight, and
  positions Universal Intelligence as the cosmic organizing principle of which Innate is the
  individualized expression within an organism. [Journal of Contemporary Chiropractic, "Innate
  Intelligence in the Philosophy of Chiropractic: A Comparative Analysis,"
  https://journal.parker.edu/article/158568-innate-intelligence-in-the-philosophy-of-chiropractic-a-comparative-analysis,
  checked 2026-09-03]
  - The phrasing **"Innate is a segment of that Intelligence which fills the universe"** is also

    https://www.azquotes.com/author/11272-Daniel_D_Palmer, checked 2026-09-03 — **unattributed

  - Other Palmer phrasings circulating in unattributed compilations, including **"Life is the

    vast difference between treating effects and adjusting the cause"** — **NOT VERIFIED** against a
    primary text or scholarly source in this pass. Do not quote as verbatim Palmer.

- **The self healing premise as Palmer stated it.** Palmer held that "the human body had ample
  natural healing power transmitted through the nervous system," and that restoring vertebral
  alignment restored health by reestablishing nerve supply. [Wikipedia, Daniel David Palmer, checked

- **Three modern readings of innate intelligence**, as identified by the *Journal of Contemporary

  2. **Metaphorical / naturalistic** — Innate is reframed as an emergent property of complex

  3. **Rejected / analytical** — Innate is treated as a historical artifact deserving scholarly
     examination rather than professional commitment. Associated with Coulter and Keating.

### 6.4 Subluxation: the several meanings of one word

- **Original Palmer meaning:** defined by D.D. and B.J. Palmer as "pressure on nerves, abnormal
  functions creating a lesion" affecting bodily action or composition. Solon Langworthy is credited
  with first using the term to describe a vertebral misalignment narrowing the passages through which
  nerves travel. [Wikipedia, Vertebral subluxation,
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertebral_subluxation, checked 2026-09-03; Wikipedia, History of
  chiropractic, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Orthopedic / medical meaning:** a significant structural displacement, a partial dislocation,
  which is visible on static imaging such as plain radiographs. Chiropractic sources state that the
  subluxation they describe is **not necessarily visible on X-rays**, which is precisely the point of
  divergence between the two usages. [Wikipedia, Vertebral subluxation, checked 2026-09-03]

- **The 1996 Association of Chiropractic Colleges consensus definition, quoted:** **"A subluxation is
  a complex of functional and/or structural and/or pathological articular changes that compromise
  neural integrity and may influence organ system function and general health."** [Wikipedia,
  Vertebral subluxation, checked 2026-09-03]

- **The World Health Organization definition, quoted:** **"A lesion or dysfunction in a joint or
  motion segment in which alignment, movement integrity and/or physiological function are altered,
  although contact between joint surfaces remains intact."** [Wikipedia, Vertebral subluxation,
  checked 2026-09-03]

- **"Vertebral subluxation complex" (VSC)** is used interchangeably with "subluxation" and "vertebral
  subluxation" in the literature. [Same source, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Statements that the construct lacks evidentiary support:**
  - **General Chiropractic Council (UK), 2010**, quoted: the vertebral subluxation complex **"is not
    supported by any clinical research evidence that would allow claims to be made that it is the
    cause of disease or health concerns."**
  - **International Chiropractic Education Collaboration / international chiropractic colleges,
    2015**, quoted: **"The teaching of the vertebral subluxation complex...is unsupported by
    evidence."**
  - The same source notes that many chiropractors have rejected the historical concept in favour of
    descriptions that more accurately characterize the joint dysfunctions they treat.
  [Wikipedia, Vertebral subluxation, checked 2026-09-03 — the exact institutional name attached to
  the 2015 statement was returned as "International Chiropractic Colleges" by the retrieval tool and
  is **NOT VERIFIED**; attribute it as a 2015 statement by an international group of chiropractic
  educators rather than naming a specific body.]

- **NOT VERIFIED / GAP:** no survey figure could be located in this pass for what proportion of
  currently practising chiropractors or current students hold a literal, a metaphorical, or a
  rejecting view of innate intelligence or of subluxation as a cause of systemic disease. Do not put
  a percentage on the distribution of these three positions anywhere in the book.

## 6. Other Professions, Integration, and Payment (added 2026-09-03 for Chapter 6)

Research pass conducted 2026-09-03 by the chapter 6 author, using the same verification conventions as the sections above. Items that could not be confirmed at primary-source level are flagged.

### 6.1 Training of the professions a chiropractor works alongside

- **Physicians (MD and DO):** A bachelor's degree, then **4 years of medical school**, then residency training. BLS states residents train **"3 to 9 years"** depending on specialty, with an additional **1 to 3 years** of fellowship for subspecialization. All states require licensure, which requires completing residency training and passing standardized national exams. BLS states MDs and DOs "use the same methods of treatment, including drugs and surgery," with DOs placing "additional emphasis on the body's musculoskeletal system, preventive medicine, and holistic (whole-person) patient care." [BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, Physicians and Surgeons, https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/physicians-and-surgeons.htm, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Osteopathic physicians (DO) specifically:** 4 years of osteopathic medical school including instruction in osteopathic manipulative treatment (OMT); licensing via COMLEX-USA; residency in the same ACGME-accredited programs as MDs; **full practice rights including prescribing and surgery in all 50 states**. On actual OMT use: a 2001 survey found **more than 50% of respondents used OMT on fewer than 5% of their patients**, and the source states "the majority of practicing physicians with a DO degree do not practice OMT in their daily work," with the number reporting consistent OMT use falling steadily even as osteopathic graduate numbers rose more than 150% between 1980 and 2005. [Wikipedia, Osteopathic medicine in the United States, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osteopathic_medicine_in_the_United_States, checked 2026-09-03 — **NOT VERIFIED** at primary-source level: the 2001 survey itself was not retrieved; the direction of the finding (most DOs rarely use OMT) is consistently reported across sources, so state it as a reported pattern rather than a precise statistic.] **NOT VERIFIED:** a specific required curricular hour count for OMT instruction in DO programs; no reliable single figure was located in this pass. Do not put a number on it.

- **Physical therapists (DPT):** The entry-level degree is the Doctor of Physical Therapy, typically **3 to 4 years after a bachelor's degree**. One 2009 survey of programs found a typical total of roughly **230.7 credit hours** (about 114.2 prerequisite credits plus 116.5 professional credits). Programs include full-time supervised clinical practice. Graduates sit a licensure examination accepted in all 50 states. **As of January 1, 2015, all 50 states and the District of Columbia allow some form of direct access to physical therapists** (that is, a patient may be seen without a physician referral, though the scope of that access varies by state). [Wikipedia, Doctor of Physical Therapy, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Physical_Therapy, checked 2026-09-03]

- **Massage therapists:** **46 states plus the District of Columbia** require a massage therapy credential; California, Kansas, Minnesota, and Wyoming lack statewide licensure. Minimum education hours range roughly **500 to 1,000 hours**, with most states falling in the **500 to 750 hour** band (500 hours at the low end in states including Florida, Texas, Arkansas, and Virginia; 1,000 hours in Nebraska and New York). The Massage and Bodywork Licensing Examination (MBLEx) is accepted by 46 of 49 regulated US jurisdictions; Hawaii and New York administer their own exams. [MBLExGuide state requirements summary, https://mblexguide.com/massage-license/, checked 2026-09-03 — a commercial exam-prep site aggregating state board requirements rather than a primary regulatory source; the 500 to 750 hour band is consistent across multiple aggregators, but verify any single state's number against that state's board before publishing it as specific to that state.]

- **Acupuncturists:** Certification eligibility through the national certification body requires a **minimum 3 year master's degree of at least 1,905 hours / 105 credits** for acupuncture. Chinese herbology certification requires additional training (a minimum of 450 hours of didactic herbal instruction plus herbal clinical training; a 4 year herbal medicine master's program totals roughly 2,625 hours across all subject areas). [NCCAOM / NCBAHM educational eligibility page, https://www.ncbahm.org/certification/nccaom-certification-eligibility/educational-eligibility/, checked 2026-09-03 — the organization's own certification page; note the domain redirected from nccaom.org to ncbahm.org during this pass, apparently reflecting an organizational renaming. **NOT VERIFIED:** state-by-state acupuncture licensure requirements, which vary and were not surveyed here.]

### 6.2 Overlap in spinal manipulation, and the boundary disputes it produces

- **Who performs joint and spinal manipulation:** "In North America and Europe, joint manipulation is most commonly performed by chiropractors (estimated to perform over 90% of all manipulative treatments), American-trained osteopathic physicians, occupational therapists, physiotherapists, and European osteopaths." Physical therapists have historically used the Maitland grading vocabulary, in which manipulation corresponds to a Grade V mobilization, so the same act appears in two professions under two names. [Wikipedia, Joint manipulation, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_manipulation, checked 2026-09-03 — the "over 90%" figure is described in the source as an estimate; treat it as approximate.]

- **CONTESTED — the Alabama physical therapist case (Dunning):** The Alabama State Board of Chiropractic Examiners sued James Dunning, a licensed physical therapist in Montgomery, in Montgomery County Circuit Court, asserting that his advertising and performance of spinal manipulation constituted the unlawful practice of chiropractic, and sought a declaration that spinal manipulation may only be performed by chiropractic licensees plus a permanent injunction. The two sides describe the outcome differently and both descriptions should be presented:
  - **Physical therapy side:** describes the trial court's dismissal as a ruling in Dunning's favor, affirmed unanimously (9 to 0) by the Supreme Court of Alabama on **January 20, 2012**, and characterizes the result as establishing that spinal manipulation falls within the physical therapy scope of practice. [Spinal Manipulation Institute, https://spinalmanipulation.org/2015/02/20/supreme-court-ruling-in-favor-of-alabama-physical-therapist-in-spinal-manipulation-case/, checked 2026-09-03 — a physical therapy advocacy source. A Quackwatch page titled "Alabama Courts Uphold Practice of Spinal Manipulation by Physical Therapists" describes the outcome the same way; the page itself is blocked to automated retrieval by robots.txt and only its title was confirmed in this pass.]
  - **Chiropractic board side:** the Alabama State Board of Chiropractic Examiners, in its own January 2013 newsletter, states the Circuit Court "declined to rule, stating it lacked authority to decide the matter," that the Alabama Supreme Court affirmed without issuing an opinion, and expressly disputes the characterization above: **"Neither the Circuit Court nor the Supreme Court by its affirmance specifically held that a physical therapist can lawfully perform spinal manipulations."** The Board maintains its position that a physical therapist performing spinal manipulation is the unlawful practice of chiropractic. [Alabama State Board of Chiropractic Examiners newsletter, https://chiro.alabama.gov/Newsletter/Newsletter_1_13.pdf, checked 2026-09-03]
  - **Assessment for the book:** present this as a live, unresolved boundary dispute described in incompatible terms by the two professions, not as a settled holding in either direction. Do not state that a court "held" that PTs may manipulate; do not state that the board "won." The verifiable core is that the suit was brought, that it was dismissed below, and that the affirmance carried no opinion.

### 6.3 Chiropractic in the Department of Veterans Affairs

- Congress **authorized VA to begin providing chiropractic care in 1999**; since **2004** the services have been available to all eligible veterans. VA doctors of chiropractic "are integrated with primary care, rehabilitation, pain management and other specialty teams" and provide "diagnosis and management of non-operative neuromuscular and musculoskeletal conditions." Care requires a referral from a VA primary care or specialty provider and is delivered either on station or through community care. **From fiscal year 2005 through 2015 the number of on-station VA chiropractic clinics increased from 27 to 65.** [VA Rehabilitation and Prosthetic Services, Chiropractic Service, https://www.rehab.va.gov/chiro/, checked 2026-09-03 — VA's own program page. **NOT VERIFIED:** the current (post-2015) clinic count, which the page does not give; do not state a present-day number.]

### 6.4 Chiropractors working inside integrated and hospital based facilities

- A descriptive survey of doctors of chiropractic working in integrated US private sector health care facilities (Salsbury et al., *Journal of Manipulative and Physiological Therapeutics*, 2018 Feb;41(2):149-155) surveyed 50 such chiropractors with a 76% response rate (n = 38). Settings reported: **hospitals 40%, multispecialty offices 21%, ambulatory clinics 16%, other 21%.** **68% were employees** and 59% received salary compensation. Median number of chiropractors per facility was 2 (range 1 to 8). **More than 60% reported co-management of patients with medical professionals**, and most shared electronic health records with medical staff. [Chiro.org / ScienceDirect summary of Salsbury et al. 2018, https://atlas.chiro.org/2018/04/integration-of-doctors-of-chiropractic-into-private-sector-health-care-facilities-in-the-united-states/, checked 2026-09-03 — **important caveat: this is a purposive sample of chiropractors already known to be working in integrated settings, not a nationally representative sample. It says nothing about what fraction of US chiropractors or US facilities are integrated. Do not use these percentages as national prevalence figures.**]

### 6.5 Medicare and insurance coverage

- **Medicare Part B covers "manual manipulation of the spine by a chiropractor to correct a vertebral subluxation"** and nothing else performed or ordered by a chiropractor: Medicare "doesn't cover other services or tests a chiropractor orders, including X-rays, massage therapy, and acupuncture." After the Part B deductible the patient owes 20% of the Medicare-approved amount. [Medicare.gov, Chiropractic services coverage, https://www.medicare.gov/coverage/chiropractic-services, checked 2026-09-03]
- Covered chiropractic manipulative treatment is billed under **CPT codes 98940, 98941, and 98942**. **"For Medicare purposes, a chiropractor must place an AT modifier on a claim when providing active/corrective treatment to treat acute or chronic subluxation"**; claims without it are considered not medically necessary. **"Once the maximum therapeutic benefit has been achieved for a given condition, ongoing maintenance therapy is not considered to be medically necessary."** Medicare therefore does not pay for maintenance care. [CMS Medicare Coverage Database, Billing and Coding: Chiropractic Services (A56273), https://www.cms.gov/medicare-coverage-database/view/article.aspx?articleId=56273, checked 2026-09-03; article revision effective 08/28/2025.]
- **The Affordable Care Act does not name chiropractic as an essential health benefit.** "Chiropractic care is not spelled out specifically in the essential health benefits requirements," so coverage on individual and small group plans "will depend on the state's benchmark plan and/or benefit mandates." Where covered, coverage is typically "limited to a set number of visits per year," is "based on medical necessity," and maintenance or wellness care is "generally not covered." [healthinsurance.org FAQ, https://www.healthinsurance.org/faqs/are-visits-to-the-chiropractor-or-physical-therapist-covered-under-the-affordable-care-act/, checked 2026-09-03 — a consumer-facing insurance explainer, not a regulatory primary source; the underlying benchmark-plan data is published by CMS at https://www.cms.gov/marketplace/resources/data/essential-health-benefits. **NOT VERIFIED:** any count of how many states mandate chiropractic coverage.]
